Another strike story this week, after [WestJet’s free change-or-cancel window ahead of a possible cabin-crew strike](/news/westjet-free-change-cancel-cabin-crew-strike/). This one is in Europe: French cabin-crew unions at easyJet filed a **strike notice** after talks with the airline collapsed on **July 23rd, 2026**. That means workers can strike during the rest of July and August at easyJet’s French bases. Flights aren’t being canceled just yet. No firm strike days have been published, and easyJet has not posted a list of grounded departures tied to this notice.

If you have booked on easyJet to or from France this summer, keep an eye on this. Summer Europe has a habit of turning news into real disruptions.

## What a strike notice means in France

In French aviation, a préavis (strike notice) is the legal step that lets workers walk out during a covered window. Crew usually still have to confirm participation about **48 hours** before a given day, so cancellations (if any) tend to show up in waves rather than as one airline-wide grounding on day one.

easyJet said it was “very disappointed,” that it had already offered adjustments, and that more talks are planned for **September**. It also said it would contact passengers directly if flights are disrupted. Until they do contact you, assume the published timetable still stands.

The dispute, as unions describe it, is about roster instability, last-minute changes, fatigue, and night flying.

## Why summer Europe keeps producing this kind of headline

In Western Europe in July and August, strike notices turn into walkouts often enough that it is worth planning for the chance of disruption.

French airspace is the clearest example. On **July 3rd and 4th, 2025**, [EUROCONTROL](https://www.eurocontrol.int/publication/impact-french-atc-strike-3-4-july-2025-european-aviation) found French air traffic control strikes affected **more than 1 million** passengers, with about **200,000** unable to fly as planned because of cancellations. Averaged across those two days, that was about **1,422** cancellations and **3,713** delayed flights **per day**, with knock-on delay into Spain, the UK, and Italy. EUROCONTROL put the two-day industry cost around **€120 million** (~US$130 million / ~CA$180 million).

That was controllers, not easyJet cabin crew. Still, the lesson for travelers is the same: in peak summer, France has a long track record of aviation strikes, and a notice can wreck a vacation if enough people walk out.

## How easyJet France’s own 2026 notices have gone so far

This is not the first easyJet France cabin-crew dispute this year. Unions already called for walkouts around **New Year’s Day** and again on **Easter Monday, April 6th, 2026**, covering the same six French bases: **Paris Orly, Paris Charles de Gaulle, Nice, Nantes, Lyon, and Bordeaux**.

April is useful context because the warnings sounded worse than what happened. easyJet told AFP it canceled **three** rotations that day (two from Orly, one from Lyon) and still planned to operate most France-related flying. A notice, or even a called strike day, can still leave most flights flying if enough crew show up and the airline covers the gaps.

No need to rip up your ticket the second a préavis appears, but don’t ignore it either. How bad it gets depends on how many people strike, which days they pick, and how full August already is.

## What this means for travelers

If your ticket is on easyJet **to, from, or within France** in late July or August, you are the group that could get hit. Connecting onto an easyJet flight at those bases can get messy too, even if you arrived in Europe on another airline.

If you are only overflying France and you are **not** on an easyJet flight, this cabin-crew notice does not apply to you.

I’m in Europe again this summer: Bansko, then Mallorca, then over to the US. No strike has wrecked those plans so far. When news like this pops up, I still like having flexible dates if I can, and I check my bookings to make sure I haven’t been affected.

## What to do now

1. Confirm whether you are actually booked on **easyJet** into or out of the French bases above.
2. Turn on easyJet app/email/SMS alerts and check [Manage Bookings](https://www.easyjet.com/en/manage/viewbooking) plus [flight status](https://www.easyjet.com/en/help/boarding-and-flying/delays-and-cancellations) before you leave for the airport on travel day.
3. If your plans can move, look at change options now, while seats still exist on other days or carriers. Waiting until August cancellations start is a worse time to shop.
4. If easyJet cancels your flights, use the airline’s rebooking/refund path first. Care (meals, hotels when owed) can still apply under EU rules even when cash compensation is disputed for some strike cases. Read easyJet’s [notice of rights](https://www.easyjet.com/en/terms-and-conditions/notice-of-rights-for-flight-delays-and-cancellations) against what actually happened to your flight.
5. Leave extra buffer around tight same-day connections, cruises, and non-refundable hotels on peak weekends.

## Bottom line

French easyJet cabin-crew unions filed a summer strike notice after July 23rd talks failed. Watch your booking through August, and keep some flexibility if you can. Until easyJet cancels your flight or emails you, the original schedule is still valid.
